# Clinical Practice Note: Management of Stage 1 Hypertension

**Patient encounter — primary care follow-up**
Document type: clinical note (de-identified, synthetic; for smoke-test fixture use only)

---

## Subjective

The patient is a 58-year-old presenting for a six-month hypertension follow-up.
Since the last visit, the patient reports occasional morning headaches and mild
fatigue but no chest pain, palpitations, dyspnea on exertion, peripheral edema,
or syncopal episodes. Medication adherence has been good. Home blood pressure
monitoring records were brought to the visit and reviewed.

The patient has no recent changes to diet, exercise, or sleep. They report
moderate alcohol intake (two to three drinks per week), no tobacco use, and a
recent attempt to increase walking to thirty minutes most days. Family history
remains notable for early cardiovascular disease in a first-degree relative.

## Objective

In-clinic measurements taken after five minutes of quiet seated rest, on the
non-dominant arm with an appropriately sized cuff. The patient's average
systolic blood pressure across three readings was 138 mmHg with a diastolic of
86 mmHg. Heart rate was 72 and regular. BMI was 28.4. The remainder of the
focused cardiovascular examination was unremarkable: no carotid bruits, normal
S1 and S2 without murmurs or gallops, no jugular venous distention, and no
peripheral edema. Recent laboratory values from the patient's home draw kit
were within the expected ranges for renal function, electrolytes, and lipids.

## Assessment

Stage 1 hypertension, partially controlled on current monotherapy. The patient
meets the diagnostic criteria for sustained hypertension based on both
in-office and out-of-office measurements, with consistent elevation above the
130/80 threshold across multiple visits. The presence of an early
cardiovascular family history places this patient in a higher absolute risk
category than the office numbers alone would suggest.

## Plan

We discussed treatment intensification and reviewed the rationale for adding
a second agent versus increasing the current dose. Given the patient's
preference to minimize medication burden, we elected to first attempt to
optimize lifestyle factors over the next eight weeks before adding a second
pharmacologic agent. The patient agreed to a structured walking program, a
DASH-style dietary trial with reduced sodium, and weight reduction targeting
a five-percent decrease over three months.

First-line treatment for hypertension at this stage typically includes lifestyle
modification combined with ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers,
both of which reduce vasoconstriction by acting on the renin-angiotensin
system. ACE inhibitors block the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II,
producing vasodilation, reducing arterial wall tension, and lowering both
preload and afterload. Calcium channel blockers and thiazide diuretics remain
appropriate alternatives. Beta-blockers are not first-line for uncomplicated
hypertension but remain valuable when a concurrent indication exists.

If home blood pressure averages do not fall below 130/80 within eight weeks of
lifestyle optimization, we will add a second agent at the next visit. Given
this patient's profile, a low-dose thiazide added to the existing ACE inhibitor
is the most likely next step. We discussed potential adverse effects including
mild cough with ACE inhibitors, electrolyte shifts with thiazides, and the
importance of reporting any new symptoms promptly.

Regular monitoring is essential for patients with comorbidities or elevated
baseline risk. The patient was instructed to continue daily home blood pressure
measurements, recording morning and evening readings, and to bring the log to
the next visit. We reviewed proper measurement technique, including cuff size,
arm support, and the value of repeated readings after a brief rest. The patient
demonstrated good understanding of the plan and was provided with written
materials summarizing the diagnosis, the treatment goals, and the warning signs
that would warrant earlier follow-up — including persistent headache,
visual changes, chest discomfort, or readings consistently above 160/100.

Follow-up appointment scheduled in eight weeks. Telephone outreach by the
clinical pharmacist in four weeks to assess adherence and tolerability.
